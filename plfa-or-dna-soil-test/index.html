PLFA or DNA soil test: which one answers your question?

A PLFA test tells you how much living microbial biomass your soil holds and how it divides into broad groups. A DNA test tells you which species are there. Neither replaces the other.

This page explains both. The facts about the best-known European PLFA test, the Eurofins Soil Life Monitor, come from Eurofins' own pages, with the link and the date we read them. We sell a DNA test, BioFiling, and we say below where PLFA is the better choice.

What a PLFA test measures

PLFA stands for phospholipid fatty acids, parts of the membranes of living cells. They break down quickly once a cell dies, so the result reflects living organisms. Eurofins cites a half-life of 14 to 27 hours [2].

The Soil Life Monitor reports total microbial biomass, bacterial and fungal biomass, the ratio of fungi to bacteria, protozoa and other groups, together with organic matter and pH [1][2]. Eurofins describes the routine measurement as near-infrared spectroscopy calibrated against a laboratory reference method, GC-MS [1][2].

What it cannot do is name species. Eurofins says this itself: the PLFA method distinguishes groups of micro-organisms, and plate methods and DNA techniques are more suitable for identifying specific species [2][3]. A PLFA result cannot tell you whether Fusarium oxysporum is in the field.

What a DNA test measures

A DNA analysis sequences marker genes of the fungi and bacteria in the sample and reports the species found. In one real BioFiling report that meant 753 species in a single sample, 18 of them plant pathogens, five of those Fusarium species, each with its quantity, together with the antagonist organisms that act against them. Open the full report.

Its limit is the reverse of PLFA's strength. DNA shows that an organism is present, not that it is alive and active. A study in Nature Microbiology found that on average about 40% of the prokaryotic and fungal DNA in soil was extracellular or came from cells that were no longer intact [6]. For the amount of living biomass, PLFA is the more direct measure.

Do you need both?

Sometimes. Use PLFA to follow the quantity of soil life over the years. Use DNA when a decision depends on which organisms are there: the choice of crop or variety, a rotation, biological crop protection, or buying or renting land. The same field can carry both tests because they answer different questions.
