Can you test soil for Fusarium and other pathogens before you sow?

Yes. A DNA analysis of a soil sample names the fungi and bacteria present, species by species, including the ones that cause wilt, root rot and leaf spot. This page shows what one real report found, what the figures mean, and where the method stops.

What one real sample showed

In one soil sample the analysis identified 753 species. Eighteen of them are plant pathogens: 14 fungi and 4 bacteria. Five are Fusarium species. These are eight of the eighteen, exactly as the report lists them:

Which pathogens the test covers

The analysis reads marker genes of the fungi and bacteria in the sample and reports the species it finds. It is not limited to a list fixed in advance. Reports name fungal pathogens, oomycetes and bacterial pathogens, and mark each one as specific to certain crops or as a universal pathogen.

If one organism matters most to you, for example Verticillium dahliae before potatoes or strawberries, tell us before you order and we confirm how it is covered.

What the figures mean, and what they do not

  • A higher figure means more DNA of that species in the sample. You can compare organisms within one sample, and the same field from year to year. The report explains the calculation: 250 mg of soil is analysed and the count is scaled to 1 kg.
  • DNA shows presence, not active disease. Whether a crop becomes diseased depends on its susceptibility, the amount of the pathogen, the weather and the organisms that suppress it. That is why the report carries a written interpretation and not only a list.
  • Soil holds DNA from dead cells too. A study in Nature Microbiology found that on average about 40% of the prokaryotic and fungal DNA in soil was extracellular or came from cells that were no longer intact. A DNA result is a signal to weigh, not a verdict.
  • A sample describes the soil it came from. A field with different soils or histories needs a sample from each zone. We send sampling instructions for your location with the order confirmation.

Source: Carini et al., "Relic DNA is abundant in soil and obscures estimates of soil microbial diversity", Nature Microbiology, 2016, doi:10.1038/nmicrobiol.2016.242.

A targeted test is a good choice when you already know which pathogen you are looking for. Whole-community sequencing fits when you do not know yet, or when you also want to see what in the soil works against the pathogen.

When it pays to test

  • Before a susceptible, high-value crop: vegetables, potatoes, berries, a new orchard or vineyard.
  • When a field underperforms and the chemistry does not explain it.
  • Before you buy or rent land.
  • Before you spend on biological crop protection, to see which antagonists the soil already holds.
